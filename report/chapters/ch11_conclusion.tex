\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{ch:conclusion}

\textbf{Contribution.} TrustLens is a research prototype that imports a Hugging Face text-classification model, runs five probes locally, stores the evidence under content hashes, turns it into O/S/D ratings and produces a versioned report whose primary output is a five-dimensional audit profile. Its distinguishing design choice is to keep measured evidence, risk decisions and ratings apart, and to withhold a rating when the evidence is insufficient.

\textbf{Main findings.} Measured evidence was reproducible and tracked injected severity. Separating documentation gaps from observed problems removed false flags on clean controls. A rule on the gap in true-positive rates flagged every injected disparity at label-flip rates of 0.4 and above on fresh data, and the hash comparison against a manifest caught a one-byte change in a controlled copy (in the Hub audit the manifest covered one weight file per model, and a match shows agreement with the reference, not safety). Reviewers agreed most on whether the evidence was sufficient and which dimension it concerned. The central lesson is that evidence, risk decisions and ratings are different things: evidence was reliable, but the risk and score layers did not inherit that reliability, and improving one layer did not improve the others. \textbf{Open questions.} Three results did not come out as planned: the prespecified robustness sweep withheld every rating, the LLM comparison stayed inconclusive, and the fairness rule flagged the real reference model, which does have a measurable disparity. The scores separate synthetic defect levels but are not calibrated to real-world harm. Section~\ref{ch:limits} lists the full set of limits.

\textbf{Intended use and future work.} TrustLens helps a human reviewer see where evidence is strong and where it is thin, with every number traceable to stored evidence. The next priorities are to validate the evidence-to-rating step and the later evidence guards on new data and against external judgments of harm, to repeat the LLM comparison with one pinned provider, to test mention-based and demographic groups, to collect larger reviewer panels, and to add broader probes without losing the separation of layers.
