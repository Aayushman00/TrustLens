\chapter{Experimental Design}\label{ch:method}

Each experiment is defined once, here; Chapter~\ref{ch:results} refers back. Plans for the scoring-method evaluation, the LLM comparison, the fairness confirmation, the severity sweeps and the independent review were written before the runs; failed hypotheses are reported as failed; humans and LLMs reading evidence packets are \emph{evidence reviewers}, not ground truth. Deviations that affect interpretation are flagged where they arise.

\section{Common setup}\label{sec:setup}
\textbf{Task and data.} All behavioural experiments use binary toxicity classification of English comments. The evaluation set is a 3\,000-row sample of the Civil Comments test split \cite{borkan2019} with a 20\% toxic rate (toxicity $\ge0.5$) and 282 severe positive rows (Section~\ref{sec:safety}). The group flag (dataset column \code{identity\_ref}) is 1 when the annotator-averaged identity-attack score exceeds 0.0001. It is a toxicity-subtype signal, \emph{not} a count of identity mentions (no mention-based rerun was performed), and it is strongly tied to the toxicity label (label-rate gap 0.505 between groups in this evaluation set). The injected defect and the metric use the same flag, so the experiment is internally consistent but measures disparity across this flag only, not fairness for protected identity groups.

\textbf{Models.} Evaluation rows come from the Civil Comments test split and training rows from its train split, so no controlled model is evaluated on text it was trained on (the training data of the Hub models may overlap with the evaluation rows, which we could not check); the fresh data of the confirmation and fitting experiments were additionally filtered to have no exact text in common with any other slice. Controlled models are fine-tuned from \code{bert-base-uncased} \cite{devlin2019} on an 8\,000-row clean slice (3 epochs, seed 42; remaining settings are in the reproduction guide). The reference \code{unitary/toxic-bert} has six sigmoid outputs; in our first experiment its label mapping sent ``toxic'' to the ``severe toxic'' output, so the model never predicted toxic yet looked perfectly fair and robust. This is why the constant-predictor gate exists and why we built a 2-label reference (same encoder, a new head that predicts ``toxic'' exactly when the original toxic output exceeds 0.5; accuracy 0.868, TPR gap 0.174), which has its own measurable disparity (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}).

\textbf{Statistics.} The study is descriptive. Confidence intervals come from two methods, both with a 95\% level. The percentile bootstrap \cite{efron1979} re-samples the evaluation rows with replacement, recomputes the statistic on each re-sample and reads the interval from the percentiles of the results. TrustLens uses it for the TPR gap (EOD) and for the accuracy drop under typos. The Wilson interval \cite{wilson1927} is a closed-form interval for a single proportion and is used for the severe false-negative rate and for clean accuracy. Both reflect sampling of rows from one evaluation set under fixed trained models. They say nothing about how results would change if a model were retrained or the data were drawn differently. A 95\% interval is a statement about the procedure's long-run coverage, not a 95\% probability for the one interval reported. Spearman's $\rho$ \cite{spearman1904} is a rank correlation: it is $+1$ when a metric rises at every step across ordered levels (such as label-flip rates), $-1$ when it falls at every step, and says nothing about the size of the steps. Reviewer agreement is the pairwise agreement rate plus chance-corrected measures: Cohen's $\kappa$ \cite{cohen1960} (two raters), Fleiss' $\kappa$ \cite{fleiss1971} (many raters) and Krippendorff's $\alpha$ \cite{krippendorff2004} (ordinal ratings such as a 1--9 severity). All three ask how much raters agree beyond what guessing with the same answer frequencies would give: 0 is chance level and 1 is perfect agreement. They matter here because when 76 of 84 answers are ``yes'', raw agreement is high almost by default. Bootstrap intervals use $B=1000$ resamples in the probes and $B=5000$ over packets. No significance tests are run; 12 packets, five levels and seven models do not support them.

\textbf{Why each defect.} Each defect must be injectable at a known size, so that the ground truth is the construction and not an opinion. V1 relabels a share of the non-toxic training comments that carry the group flag as toxic, which is how annotation bias would reach a model. V2 trains on a small, narrow set for longer and at a higher learning rate, so that it overfits and should be more sensitive to character noise; its first design collapsed into a majority-class predictor and was replaced (a decision made after seeing data). V5 relabels half of the severe toxic comments as non-toxic, so the model lets the most harmful comments through. The documentation defects have no behavioural effect by design: V3 is clean weights behind a skeleton card; V4 and V4b attach an overclaiming card to weights copied byte for byte, and V4b exists because V4 shares weights with V1, which would confound Integrity with Fairness. V6 combines the fairness and safety flips with a skeleton card. The integrity demonstration flips one byte of a copy of V1's weights and compares it with the original's manifest.

V3, V4 and V4b are documentation flaws around ordinary weights, and V4 is behaviourally identical to V1, which limits what ``detection'' can mean for them. Six further models vary one knob for the severity experiments: label-flip rates for Fairness (0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.7, 1.0) and Safety (0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0). The flips are nested: every example relabelled at a lower defect level is also relabelled at higher levels, so models differ only in the amount of corruption.

\textbf{Detection rules, fixed before the runs.} At \emph{score level}, a dimension is flagged if its mean score over repeats lies below the mean of the 2-label reference by more than $\max(1.0,\,2\times\text{pooled std})$; a dimension without a score is withheld, not flagged. At \emph{evidence level}, a dimension is flagged if, in a majority of runs, the probe reports a non-empty risk list or a risk-detected status (for Safety also if the Wilson lower bound of the severe FNR exceeds the control's point estimate). A false positive is a flagged dimension that was not injected; controls are reported through a \emph{control flag rate}. The suite ran five times per model and the sweeps three times.

\section{Fairness confirmation run}\label{sec:fairconf}
Because the TPR-gap rule was chosen after inspecting exploratory data, a confirmation was planned and documented before it ran, with the rule, $\varepsilon=0.02$, width limit 0.15 and $B=1000$ unchanged. A new data seed gives a fresh 3\,000-row evaluation set (20\% toxic; 260 severe positives; group sizes 2\,500 and 500; positive-label rates 0.113 and 0.636) and a disjoint 8\,000-row training slice, with no exact-text overlap with the suite's data. Models: a clean control, label flips conditioned on the group flag at 0.2, 0.4, 0.7, 1.0 and the 2-label reference, each run three times. The hypotheses were fixed in advance, and if one failed the rule would stay unchanged and the failure be reported. \textbf{H1:} no control model is flagged. \textbf{H2:} every flip rate of 0.4 or more is flagged. \textbf{H3:} the gap rises with the flip rate, measured by Spearman's rank correlation $\rho$ between flip rate and EOD over the five levels ($\rho$ is $+1$ when the metric rises at every step and $-1$ when it falls at every step). \textbf{H4:} how often the width rule withholds a rating (descriptive).

\section{Severity sweeps}\label{sec:sweepdesign}
The production Robustness and Safety probes ran on the confirmation evaluation set, with no LLM. \emph{Robustness:} one knob, the attack budget, giving 1, 3, 5, 8 character swaps (budgets 0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.08) on clean V3 weights. \emph{Safety:} the models relabelled at rates 0 to 1.0 plus the 2-label reference as control. A \emph{violation} is an adjacent pair of levels where the evidence moves against the expected direction; ties are counted; $\rho$ is descriptive, with no pass threshold. Each level ran twice.

\section{Evaluation of the scoring method}\label{sec:calib}
The anchors of the range-scaled mapping (Table~\ref{tab:anchors}) were fitted on a separate split (seed 43; own 3\,000-row evaluation and 8\,000-row training data, disjoint by exact text from all other data; seven models with nested label flips (see Section~\ref{sec:suite}) of 0.3, 0.6, 1.0 for Fairness and Safety and attack budgets 0.02--0.10 for Robustness). The mapping was then applied, without re-measuring, to the confirmation and sweep evidence that was set aside for evaluation rather than used in the fit. These evaluation results were already known when the mapping was written, so the evaluation is constrained by the pre-declaration but not blind. It tests whether scores separate the synthetic defect levels, not whether they track real-world harm.

\section{Deterministic versus LLM interpretation}\label{sec:l2m}
Twelve evidence packets (self-contained bundles of evidence about one model) were built from controlled models (the 2-label reference, V1, a tampered copy of V1, V2--V6, V4b and Safety sweep levels 0.25, 0.75, 1.0). A packet holds the card text with the model name replaced by an identifier, the file verification and, where available, the behavioural measurement; it never contains the FRIES score, O/S/D, risks, statuses, flags or defect labels. The deterministic evaluator recomputes the evidence with the production functions; the LLM evaluator is the production assessment agent, five runs per packet (60 planned runs). Each run rates three dimensions, so the comparison unit is the packet--dimension pair (12 $\times$ 3 = 36), and agreement is computed between the deterministic answer and the modal LLM answer for each pair (Table~\ref{tab:l2denom} lists every denominator). LLM ``risk present'' means $O\le5$; the prompt gives no option to withhold, so the LLM cannot answer ``uncertain''. Fairness and Robustness never reach an LLM and are out of scope. Provider quotas stopped the first sessions after 22 valid answers. The missing calls were repeated with the same script, prompt, packets and provider chain until all 60 returned valid responses, and the answers of each provider are also analysed separately because the 60 runs come from three providers. A valid response satisfies the output schema and belongs to its intended packet and run.

\section{Independent evidence review}\label{sec:l10m}
Twelve single-dimension packets were declared before any was built: covering the five dimensions with defect, control, borderline and insufficient-evidence cases. Packets show measured fields with plain explanations of what a number \emph{is}, not what it \emph{means}. Each reviewer answers: Q1 is the evidence sufficient (yes/no/uncertain); Q2 is there evidence of a meaningful risk; Q3 which dimension; Q4 severity from 1 (catastrophic) to 9 (negligible); Q5 a rationale. The \emph{TrustLens reference answer} comes from the same evidence: risk yes iff a risk was raised, no when evidence is sufficient and no risk, uncertain when evidence is insufficient. Three evaluator groups stay separate: humans (an online form), an official LLM review through the providers, and a supplementary LLM review. Comparisons report pairwise agreement, severity exact and within one point, and $\kappa$ only with at least eight common packets. The human study used the final TrustLens reference answers; the LLM reviews were compared with an earlier set.

\section{External Hugging Face model audit}\label{sec:extm}
Seven real text-classification models were chosen deliberately to vary in architecture, size, training data, label scope, documentation and language; the selection was written before any evaluation and each model is pinned to an exact revision. The setup is the controlled suite's (same evaluation set, LLM-assisted engine; multi-label models use the toxicity output). Each model ran once; we report the final rerun.
