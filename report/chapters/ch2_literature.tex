\chapter{Literature Review}\label{ch:lit}

\section{What ``trustworthy'' has meant}
Early work treated trust as a synonym for accuracy and generalisation. Later definitions broadened it. Surveys such as Kemmerzell et~al.\ \cite{kemmerzell2025} and Mattioli et~al.\ \cite{mattioli2024} collect requirements like fairness, robustness, transparency, privacy, accountability and human oversight, and then spend a good deal of space on how hard it is to attach a number to each. Policy documents such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework \cite{nist2023} list characteristics of trustworthy systems (valid and reliable, safe, secure, accountable and transparent, explainable, privacy-enhanced, fair) while stating that trade-offs between them need human judgment. Two points recur in these sources: no single metric captures trust, and a measured property is evidence for trust, not trust itself. TrustLens adopts both as design rules, and the central framing of this report is a restatement of the second one.

Vashistha and Farahi take a more formal route. Their I-trustworthy framework ties trust to calibration, so a model is trusted when its confidence reflects its competence \cite{vashistha2025}, and their U-trustworthy work argues that expected utility, not calibration alone, is the right notion \cite{vashistha2024}. Both concern probabilistic classifiers. They do not cover documentation, provenance or the integrity of the files someone downloads, which is where much of the practical risk on a public hub sits.

\section{The FRIES score}
Rutinowski et~al.\ \cite{rutinowski2024} propose FRIES as a trust score for machine-learning systems. It borrows the structure of Failure Mode and Effects Analysis: for each risk an analyst rates Occurrence, Severity and Detection, a per-risk score is computed from the three ratings, and the scores are aggregated per dimension and then across the five dimensions (Fairness, Robustness, Integrity, Explainability, Safety). The paper gives a formula and a procedure, but leaves open how a concrete measurement, say a parity gap of 0.17 with a wide interval, becomes an integer rating. We treat that gap as the main research problem of the implementation side of the project, and Chapter~\ref{ch:fries} is mostly about our attempts to close it and how far they got.

\section{Measures behind each dimension}
\textbf{Fairness.} Group-fairness criteria compare a model's behaviour across groups. Demographic parity compares positive-prediction rates; equalized odds and equal opportunity, introduced by Hardt et~al.\ \cite{hardt2016}, compare true- and false-positive rates, or true-positive rates alone. For toxicity classification, Borkan et~al.\ \cite{borkan2019} showed that models trained on comments can pick up identity terms as cues, and released the Civil Comments data with identity annotations that we use. Mehrabi et~al.\ \cite{mehrabi2021} survey the many ways bias enters a pipeline. An important caveat for our experiments is that demographic parity cannot tell an unfair model from a fair model facing groups with different base rates. We hit this in practice (Section~\ref{sec:l4}), and it is why the final rule uses the true-positive-rate gap.

\textbf{Robustness.} Character-level perturbations are a classic cheap attack on text models. HotFlip \cite{ebrahimi2018} uses gradient-guided character flips, and behavioural testing frameworks such as CheckList \cite{ribeiro2020} organise perturbation tests by capability. The TrustLens probe is far simpler than either: it swaps a few characters at random and measures the accuracy drop. It measures sensitivity to typos, not adversarial robustness.

\textbf{Explainability and transparency.} Feature-attribution methods explain individual predictions. A different, documentation-centred line starts from model cards \cite{mitchell2019} and datasheets \cite{gebru2021}, which propose structured disclosure of intended use, limitations, training data and evaluation. The TrustLens Explainability probe follows this second line: it looks at what a card discloses, and does not look at model internals.

\textbf{Safety.} We use the word in a narrow behavioural sense for the toxicity setting: how often the model misses \emph{severely} toxic content, compared with a reference model on the same data. The card-based part of the probe only checks whether misuse, privacy and security disclosures exist.

\section{Supply-chain and provenance concerns}
Pre-trained model reuse creates a software supply chain. Jiang et~al.\ \cite{jiang2023reuse} describe how Hugging Face models are reused and note risks around missing or inaccurate documentation and attribution. At the extreme, a tampered checkpoint can carry a backdoor that behaves normally on clean inputs \cite{gu2017badnets}. Comparing a downloaded file with a trusted hash is the straightforward countermeasure. It proves that the bytes match a reference. It does not prove the reference is good, and our integrity design (Chapter~\ref{ch:fries}) tries to keep that distinction visible.

\section{Statistical tools used in the validation}
The validation chapters lean on a handful of standard tools. Confidence intervals for rates come from the percentile bootstrap \cite{efron1979} or, for a single proportion such as a false-negative rate, from the Wilson interval \cite{wilson1927}. Rank association between injected severity and a measurement is summarised by Spearman's $\rho$ \cite{spearman1904}; with four or five severity levels this is descriptive, not a test. Agreement between reviewers uses observed pairwise agreement, Cohen's $\kappa$ for two raters \cite{cohen1960}, Fleiss' $\kappa$ for more \cite{fleiss1971} and Krippendorff's $\alpha$ for ordinal or interval ratings \cite{krippendorff2004}. All of these are unstable with 12 items, and we say so wherever we use them.

\section{Where TrustLens sits}
We position TrustLens as an \emph{applied integration} of known pieces (group-fairness metrics, a perturbation test, a behavioural miss-rate test, card-coverage checks and artifact hashing) under the FRIES formula. We did not find a public tool that does exactly this for Hub models, but our search was not systematic, so we make no novelty claim beyond that.
