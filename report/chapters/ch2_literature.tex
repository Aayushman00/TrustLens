\chapter{Related Work}\label{ch:lit}

TrustLens sits between several bodies of work that rarely meet in one tool: trust-scoring methodologies, group-fairness toolkits, general model-evaluation suites, documentation standards, supply-chain security and audit process frameworks. This chapter takes each in turn, says what it evaluates and what it leaves out, and then states where TrustLens fits. These are neighbouring approaches with different purposes, not competing baselines, and we did not run any of the tools below against ours (Section~\ref{sec:litposition}).

\section{Trustworthiness scoring and FRIES}
Early work treated trust as accuracy and generalisation. Later surveys \cite{kemmerzell2025,mattioli2024} and policy documents such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework \cite{nist2023} list fairness, robustness, transparency, safety, accountability and human oversight, and they agree that no single metric captures trust and that trade-offs need human judgment. Vashistha and Farahi tie trust to calibration \cite{vashistha2025} and to expected utility \cite{vashistha2024}. Their frameworks are defined for probabilistic classifiers and say nothing about documentation, provenance or file integrity, which is where much of the practical risk on a public hub sits.

Rutinowski et~al.\ \cite{rutinowski2024} propose FRIES as a score inspired by Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA). For each risk an analyst rates Occurrence, Severity and Detection; per-risk scores are aggregated within each of five dimensions (Fairness, Robustness, Integrity, Explainability, Safety) and then across dimensions. The paper is a methodology. It does not say how a concrete measurement, for example a parity gap of 0.17 with a wide interval, becomes an integer rating, and it does not describe software that collects the measurements. That missing step is the main technical problem on the TrustLens side, and Chapter~\ref{ch:fries} gives our answer and its limits.

TrustML \cite{manzano2024} is the closest software relative. It is a Python package that computes composite trust indicators from a catalogue of metrics the user chooses. In the published description it works on a model and data the user already has. We found no hub import and no documentation or file-integrity metrics in that description, but we did not run the package or read its full catalogue, so this comparison rests on the paper alone.

\section{Fairness evaluation and mitigation toolkits}
Group-fairness criteria compare behaviour across groups. Demographic parity compares positive-prediction rates; equal opportunity and equalized odds \cite{hardt2016} compare true-positive and false-positive rates. Mehrabi et~al.\ \cite{mehrabi2021} survey how bias enters a machine-learning pipeline, and Borkan et~al.\ \cite{borkan2019} showed that toxicity classifiers can use identity terms as cues, which is the reason the Civil Comments data carry identity annotations.

Fairlearn \cite{weerts2023fairlearn} and AI Fairness 360 \cite{bellamy2019aif360} are the two mature open-source toolkits. According to its documentation, Fairlearn turns ordinary accuracy and error metrics into metrics reported overall and per group, and offers mitigation methods in four families (pre-processing, post-processing, reductions and adversarial mitigation). AI Fairness 360 provides fairness metrics for datasets and for models together with bias-mitigation algorithms that act on data, on training or on predictions. Fairlearn's \code{MetricFrame} can also attach bootstrap confidence intervals to its metrics, through its \code{n\_boot} and \code{ci\_quantiles} options \cite{fairlearnci}. Both are libraries a data scientist calls on a model and sensitive attributes they already hold. They leave to the user the choice of which groups matter, what interval width is acceptable and what to do when it is not, and they are not concerned with where the model came from.

TrustLens calls neither toolkit. It implements the two quantities it needs directly and adds a decision policy around them. Confidence intervals are not new, and Fairlearn offers them. The difference is workflow: TrustLens feeds its bootstrap interval into a fixed rule that withholds the fairness decision when the interval is too wide (Table~\ref{tab:fairrule}), and it records the dataset and label mapping used. We did not test whether this policy is better than leaving the interval for the user to interpret. It implements no mitigation, because its question is whether a model someone else trained shows a disparity on the user's data, not how to repair one.

\section{Language-model and foundation-model evaluation}
Evaluation suites measure what a model can do. The original HELM study \cite{liang2023helm} evaluated 30 language models on 42 scenarios (16 of them core), measuring seven metrics for each core scenario where possible (accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity and efficiency), and released the raw prompts and completions so that results can be inspected. Those figures describe the original study and are not a description of HELM today. HELM continues as an open-source framework with official leaderboards (capabilities, safety and vision-language among them), but its repository states that it entered maintenance mode on 1 June 2026 \cite{helmrepo,helmmaint}. Under that policy new features and new leaderboard evaluations are not added, bug fixes and notable models or benchmarks are still accepted, and existing leaderboards and code stay available. Maintenance mode does not invalidate the 2022 results. It means the framework should not be assumed to track newer models, and its documentation gives no single current count of models or scenarios. The Language Model Evaluation Harness \cite{gao2024harness} is a unified framework that, according to its repository README as consulted on 28 October 2026 \cite{harnessrepo}, offers over 60 standard academic benchmarks with hundreds of subtasks and variants, run through several inference backends including Hugging Face \code{transformers} and vLLM. We did not check that figure independently of the README.

These suites compare models on shared tasks, so that a score is meaningful relative to other models. TrustLens audits one classifier on the reviewer's own dataset and reports absolute evidence with its uncertainty and no leaderboard position. It also collects evidence that benchmark suites do not: what the card discloses and which files were evaluated. For robustness in particular, the field ranges from adversarial attacks such as HotFlip \cite{ebrahimi2018} to behavioural test suites such as CheckList \cite{ribeiro2020}; the TrustLens probe is far narrower, swapping a few random characters to measure typo sensitivity.

\section{Model cards and dataset documentation}
Model cards \cite{mitchell2019} and datasheets for datasets \cite{gebru2021} proposed structured disclosure: intended use, evaluation, limitations and ethical considerations for a model; motivation, composition and collection for a dataset. Data statements \cite{bender2018} and Data Cards \cite{pushkarna2022} extend the idea for language data and for dataset-level transparency. These are documentation standards. They tell an author what to write and a reader what to look for, and they do not verify anything.

The state of practice on the Hugging Face Hub is uneven. In a systematic analysis of 32\,111 model cards, Liang et~al.\ \cite{liang2024cards} found that most heavily downloaded models have a card, but that the sections on limitations, evaluation and environmental impact are the least often completed and the training section the most. Jiang et~al.\ \cite{jiang2023reuse} report incomplete and inconsistent metadata in the registry more broadly. This is the situation the Explainability and Safety card checks respond to. TrustLens reads a card as evidence about disclosure (which required sections exist, whether claims contradict the licence or each other) and keeps a missing section apart from a demonstrated problem (Section~\ref{sec:gaps}). It cannot judge whether the content of a section is accurate.

\section{Provenance, artifact integrity and supply-chain risk}
Reusing a pre-trained model is a software supply-chain decision. A downloaded checkpoint can carry a hidden behaviour that the original author did not intend, as BadNets showed for outsourced training \cite{gu2017badnets}. Jiang et~al.\ \cite{jiang2023reuse} document how widely pre-trained models are reused through the Hub and the documentation and attribution risks that follow. There is also a plain file-format risk. The Hub's documentation explains that PyTorch weights are commonly stored as Python pickle files, that loading a pickle can execute arbitrary code, and that the Hub runs antivirus and pickle-import scans on uploads while stating that this scan is not foolproof \cite{hfpickle}. The same page points to \code{safetensors} as a format designed not to execute code on load.

Signing is the stronger remedy. The OpenSSF model-signing project builds on Sigstore \cite{sigstoremodel} to sign a manifest of file hashes, so that a verifier can check that the files are unchanged and who signed them; it attests the artifacts and their signer, not what the model does.

TrustLens sits one level below that. It compares the files listed in a reference manifest with their reference hashes, which for Hub models is the sha256 the Hub publishes for each file, and reports agreement or disagreement. The design covers a model's whole file set, but in the external audit of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} each stored manifest held only the one weight file the worker loaded, so other weight formats in the same repositories were not hashed. For the files compared, agreement establishes that the evaluated bytes are the bytes the reference describes. Unlike a signature it does not tell the reviewer who produced the reference, and it does not scan for malicious pickle content. Section~\ref{sec:provenance} states what the check does and does not establish.

\section{Audit processes and integrated workflows}
Raji et~al.\ \cite{raji2020} describe an end-to-end process for internal algorithmic auditing in which each stage produces documents that feed a final report. Mökander et~al.\ \cite{mokander2023} argue for auditing language models at three levels, covering governance, the model itself and the applications built on it. Both are process frameworks; they say what an audit should contain and how responsibility is assigned, not how to produce a given measurement. The NIST framework \cite{nist2023} plays the same role at the level of organisational risk management.

\paragraph{What exists, what TrustLens implements, what the experiments add.}
\emph{Already exists:} the FRIES scoring formula; group-fairness metrics and, in Fairlearn, bootstrap intervals; benchmark suites for language models; model-card templates; hash comparison and model signing; process frameworks for auditing. \emph{TrustLens implements:} a workflow that runs a selected subset of these ideas on one imported Hub classifier and one labelled dataset (a group-fairness gap, a character-perturbation test, a miss-rate test on a severe subset, card-section checks and file hashing), keeps measured evidence, risk decisions, proposed O/S/D ratings and aggregate scores as separate stored layers, records documentation gaps apart from observed risks, and withholds a rating when stated evidence requirements are not met. \emph{The experiments add:} measurements of how those layers behave under controlled defects, human review and a small external audit. We claim no new fairness or robustness algorithm, no superiority over any tool above, and no validated trustworthiness certification.

The contribution therefore has two parts. The first is integration: the selected evidence-gathering methods run from one model reference and one dataset, record what they used, and end in a versioned report with the reviewer's step made explicit. The second is empirical. With controlled defects of known severity we measured how the evidence, the risk decisions and the proposed scores behave, which of those layers respond and which do not, and where the evidence is too uncertain to support a decision. Our search for related tools was not systematic, we did not compare TrustLens with any of them, and we therefore make no claim that no other tool does the same.
