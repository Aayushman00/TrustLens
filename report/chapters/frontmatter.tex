\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
Downloading a classifier from a public model hub takes one line of code. Deciding whether it deserves to be used takes much longer, and the information at hand is thin: a free-text model card, a licence field that may be empty, and no standard evidence about how the model behaves on sensitive groups. \textbf{TrustLens} is a semi-automated framework we built to collect that evidence for Hugging Face text classifiers. It is an evidence-driven, FRIES-aligned auditing framework. The primary output is a five-dimensional audit profile (Fairness, Robustness, Integrity, Explainability, Safety) in which each entry points back to hashed, stored evidence. The aggregate FRIES score, computed from Occurrence/Severity/Detection (O/S/D) ratings with a cube-root rule, is only a secondary heuristic summary; we do not present it as a validated measure of trustworthiness.

The report covers the system and the ten rounds of validation studies that we ran against it. We built controlled flawed models with defects injected into one FRIES dimension, and measured whether the evidence layer, the risk layer and the score layer each reacted. The measured evidence turned out to be repeatable (standard deviation zero over five runs for the deterministic probes) and monotone in injected severity (Spearman $\rho=1.0$ in every sweep). The decision layers were weaker: the first O/S/D bands compressed a full defect range into 1.3--1.7 score points, a documentation-based risk rule fired on every clean control, the first binary fairness rule could not see its target defect, and a pre-registered confirmatory test of the revised fairness rule failed one of its four hypotheses (an unflawed reference model was flagged). We fixed what we could (a calibrated mapping, a split between disclosure gaps and risks, an equal-opportunity rule, artifact-set integrity checks) and report the fixes together with what they did not solve.

An attempt to compare deterministic and LLM-based interpretation was blocked by provider outages: only 22 of 60 planned LLM runs returned an answer from the planned providers. The remaining 38 were supplied by a supplementary LLM in a later session and are labelled as such; across all 60 the LLM shows low agreement with the deterministic engine and a strong provider effect. An evidence-review study with seven form submissions on twelve blinded packets showed high raw agreement on dimension and evidence sufficiency (the latter inflated by near-uniform YES answers), moderate agreement on risk presence, and a systematic difference from TrustLens: reviewers called a risk on four packets where TrustLens did not, and TrustLens abstained on two where reviewers saw sufficient evidence. Reviewers are evidence readers, not ground truth, and six of the seven submissions share one reviewer code. Finally, seven pinned real Hub models were audited and reported as descriptive profiles without ranking; two received no FRIES score because of abstentions.

Our conclusions are deliberately narrow. TrustLens is reproducible, tracks injected severity in its evidence, and exposes where evidence is insufficient. It does not certify any model, and its aggregate score should not be read as a universal trust measure.

\textbf{Keywords:} model auditing, trustworthy AI, FRIES, Hugging Face Hub, fairness, equal opportunity, artifact integrity, model cards, evidence-driven evaluation.
