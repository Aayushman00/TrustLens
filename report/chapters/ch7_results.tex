\chapter{Results}\label{ch:results}

Every number is read from a stored result file (folder map in \code{docs/REPRODUCING\_THESIS\_RESULTS.md}). Each section states what was tested, the essential result, its meaning and any exception. We separate three layers wherever the data allow: the \emph{evidence} (what the probe measured), the \emph{risk decision} (risk, documentation gap, or withheld) and the \emph{score}.

\section{Repeatability of the measurements (RQ1)}\label{sec:repeat}
\textbf{Tested:} the nine-model controlled suite, five runs per model. \textbf{Result:} Fairness, Robustness and behavioural Safety scores have a standard deviation of 0.000 over five runs for every model (27 model-by-dimension cells), and the flags based directly on the measured results agreed across runs in 100\% of cases. The LLM-rated Integrity and Explainability scores had a mean standard deviation of 0.64 and a maximum of 2.23 (on a control). In 34 of 45 runs the LLM answered; 11 used the rule-based fallback (rate limits, three network failures), and 14 of 15 runs of an earlier real-model audit did because a daily token quota was exhausted. \textbf{Meaning:} measured evidence is reproducible to the last digit; LLM-rated dimensions are noisier and depend on provider availability.

\section{Why the original rules missed the defects: error analysis}\label{sec:errors}
Sections~\ref{sec:detect} and~\ref{sec:gapres} report \emph{that} the first rules failed. This section asks why, because each failure has a different cause and a different remedy.

\textbf{The fairness band could not see the defect.} The original Fairness band used the larger of the demographic-parity difference and the equalized-odds difference. In this evaluation set the two groups differ in toxicity rate by 0.505, so the parity difference of any model contains a large share that comes from the data and not from the model. The band gave V1 the same rating (8.00) as both reference models, so by score V1 could not be told from its control. A trigger built on the gap between groups partly measures a property of the data. The rule that conditions on the true label (the TPR gap) removes this confound, which is why it separated the injected models on fresh data. A second cause was the choice of control. In the suite, the fairness control was an external model, the 2-label conversion of \code{toxic-bert}, whose own TPR gap (0.174) was larger than that of V1 (0.164). The confirmation used a clean model trained like the others (gap 0.106). A control that differs from the defect model in more than the defect cannot isolate it.

\textbf{The safety band rounded the difference away.} V5 missed 44\% of severe toxic comments and the control 36\%. Under the original band, $O=\mathrm{scale}(1-\text{severe FNR})$ rounds both to 6, and $S$ was 6 for both because the FNR ratio did not exceed 1.5, so the dimension scores were identical (6.60) although the probe had measured the difference. The information was lost in the rating stage, not in the measurement.

\textbf{The robustness threshold lay above the defect.} The overfit V2 lost 0.041 accuracy under the character test, and the risk rule needs a drop of at least 0.05. The score, which is continuous, noticed (7.56 against 8.65), and the risk rule did not. The threshold is a fixed practical tolerance and was not adjusted after seeing V2. The result says the defect was real but modest, and that a threshold above it is silent about it by design.

\textbf{Documentation rules flagged clean controls because they read absence as evidence.} The two references have real published cards that lack some required sections and any disclosure on misuse, privacy or security, so a rule that raised a risk for a missing section flagged every model with an ordinary card (Safety and Explainability control flag rate 1.00; Integrity 0.50, because a local folder has no Hub revision to pin). The rule answered a different question (is the card complete?) from the experiment's (is there a defect?).

\textbf{What separating gaps from risks traded.} Across the three card-based dimensions, the share of non-injected cells that were flagged fell from 20 of 29 to 2 of 29, and the share of injected cases detected fell from 10 of 10 to 4 of 10 (Table~\ref{tab:l3}). The first number is a reduction in false alarms and the second the price. The recall before the change was not real, since the controls were flagged at the same rate. The recall afterwards is the part that positive evidence, a contradiction in a card or a measured miss rate, can support. What is lost is any detection of a purely documentary defect, such as the overclaiming card of V4b, from measurements. That detection moved to the reviewer's disclosure-gap list, where it is visible and not rated.

\section{Evidence, risk and score against injected severity}\label{sec:severity}
\textbf{Tested:} whether each layer responds in proportion to defect severity (RQ2), first on the suite sweeps with the original scoring bands, then with the range-scaled mapping applied to evaluation results that were set aside for checking the scoring method rather than fitting it.

\textbf{Result, evidence:} the evidence tracks severity in every family (Spearman $\rho=1.0$, so the metric rose at every step): the TPR gap rose 0.07$\to$0.32, the severe FNR 0.27$\to$0.66 and the robustness drop rose across its sweep. \textbf{Result, score:} under the original bands the dimension score moved only 1.3--1.7 points over the full range, with ties and constant components. Under the range-scaled mapping (Figure~\ref{fig:v3v4}) the range widened from 0.68--1.18 to 4.60--6.65 points (Fairness 0.68$\to$4.60, with two of five levels withheld; Robustness 0.70$\to$6.65; Safety 1.18$\to$6.00), and the score correlation with severity went from $\rho=-0.87$, $-0.95$, $-0.97$ with one tie per family to $-1.0$ with none. No score moved against the expected direction and mapping failures were zero.

\textbf{Meaning and exceptions:} nothing is lost at the evidence layer, but the \emph{risk} decision does not distinguish between different levels of Robustness or Safety defect severity (Section~\ref{sec:l6}). The wider score range is largely what a fit to the injected defects is built to produce: it shows that scores change consistently across synthetic severity levels and seeds, not that they are calibrated to harm.

\textbf{All three layers on one axis.} Figure~\ref{fig:layers} puts the evidence, the risk decision and the score of each family side by side. The pattern is the same in all three. The evidence rises with severity at every step, and the range-scaled score falls with it. The risk decision does not follow: for Robustness it is the same at all four levels, for Safety it changes only at the top two of five, and for Fairness the decision is withheld at the two lowest levels and then raised at the three highest. The decision layer works as specified and is also coarse: a threshold turns a graded quantity into a yes or a no, so a reviewer who reads only flags misses the trend that the evidence and the score still show.

\section{Uncertainty and the interval-width rule}\label{sec:uncertainty}
The width rule withheld a fairness decision on three of the thirteen audits that reported an interval, the clean control and the 0.2 level of the confirmation run, and \code{toxic-bert} in the Hub audit (Table~\ref{tab:l5} and Figure~\ref{fig:hubeod}). The three withheld widths (0.153, 0.156 and 0.154) are the three widest of the thirteen. The others lie between 0.090 and 0.148, and seven of the thirteen lie within 0.01 of the limit of 0.15. The widths do not form two clusters, one precise and one not. They form a continuous spread across the threshold, and the rule cuts it at an inherited value.


\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{fig_hub_eod.pdf}
\caption{External audit: TPR gap, interval and width $w$ for each model, in selection order. Orange: risk raised; blue: disparity observed or none; grey: withheld because $w>0.15$ (only \code{toxic-bert}).}\label{fig:hubeod}
\end{figure}

Two readings of this pattern are possible, and the data do not decide between them. On the first, the rule does what it was meant to: it declines to turn an imprecise estimate into a finding. The groups contain about 280 and 320 toxic rows in the confirmation run and 269 and 331 in the audit, so intervals of this width are plausibly what a bootstrap gives on samples of this size, and a limit of 0.15 amounts to asking whether the sample is large enough. On the second, the limit is arbitrary: the clean control's interval, [0.028, 0.181], lies wholly above $\varepsilon=0.02$, so a lower-bound rule alone would have flagged it, as it did the 2-label reference.

Two consequences follow. A withheld rating is not a passing result: the clean control's gap of 0.106 has a lower bound of 0.028, and nothing in that interval suggests the absence of a disparity. And the Hub model \code{unbiased-toxic-roberta}, flagged for a gap of 0.093 whose lower bound is only 0.020, sits just across the line: its interval, 0.142 wide, passed the width rule by 0.008, while the clean control, with a larger gap, was withheld at a width of 0.153. The report therefore states the interval beside the decision (Section~\ref{sec:threats}).

\section{Artifact integrity}\label{sec:l7}
\textbf{Tested:} a tamper demonstration and real Hub models. \textbf{Result:} untouched V1 weights matched an operator-supplied reference. Flipping one byte changed the sha256 (\code{5918a851\ldots} to \code{74c230bb\ldots}), the comparison detected a byte mismatch and a risk was raised. In the earlier 15-model run and in the final seven-model audit, the weight file that was checked matched the sha256 that the Hub publishes for it in every case. In the final audit that was one file per model, the one the worker loaded. Other weight formats in the same repositories were not hashed (Section~\ref{sec:integ}). \textbf{Meaning:} hash matching verifies that the checked bytes are what the Hub says they are. It does not show that the model is benign, that its name is accurate, that it is safe or that it has no backdoor, and it says nothing about the unhashed files (Section~\ref{sec:integ}).

\section{Deterministic versus LLM interpretation}\label{sec:l2}
\textbf{Tested:} 12 evidence packets, five LLM runs each (Section~\ref{sec:l2m}). \textbf{Completion:} all 60 planned LLM calls returned valid responses, 60/60. The first sessions returned 22 valid answers and failed on the other 38 through rate limits, exhausted daily quotas and an unavailable endpoint. The 38 calls counted as missing in the first-session summary were resolved by resuming the same script with the same prompt, packets and provider chain. The log timestamps put 34 of those 38 answers on 28 October and the four provider C answers on 3 October. The first-session summary had not counted these four as valid. Every response was checked against the expected output schema, its intended packet and its run index. The failed attempts remain in the log. Two of the late re-attempts replaced a response that had failed schema validation and a call on which all providers had been unavailable. No response was edited. The 60 answers come from three providers in the pre-specified chain, here called A, B and C (names and sampling settings in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}): provider B supplied 41, provider A 15 and provider C 4. The runs were therefore not made by one model, and they span 2 to 28 October 2026. Sampling settings are also provider-specific: temperature 0.2 was recorded for providers B and C, and the temperature used by provider A was not recorded.

\textbf{Truncated input.} A valid response is not a judgment made on complete evidence. In 5 of the 60 runs, all on packet P01 (four by provider B, one by provider A), TrustLens cut the prompt text before the call because of its fixed length limits (5\,000 characters for the model card, 3\,000 per evidence block). The stored flag does not say which cut applied, but the P01 card has 11\,095 characters, so at least the card was shortened. These five responses satisfy the output schema and count towards 60/60. They are neither failed calls nor malformed answers, but their ratings rest on less evidence than the other 55. P01 contributes three packet--dimension pairs, so every comparison that includes P01 (the pooled and the provider A and B columns of Table~\ref{tab:l2denom}) may be affected, not a single statistic. We did not estimate the effect or recompute the figures without these runs.

The section reports three kinds of quantity, and each has its own unit. \textbf{Completion} counts calls. A \emph{run} is one LLM call on one packet. Each run returns O/S/D ratings for the three dimensions an LLM sees (Integrity, Explainability and the card part of Safety), so 12 packets $\times$ 5 runs give 60 runs and 180 dimension ratings. A figure such as 22/60 says how many calls returned an answer and is not an agreement rate.

\textbf{Baseline agreement} asks whether the LLM matches the deterministic layer. The unit is the \emph{pair}, one packet--dimension combination (12 $\times$ 3 = 36). The LLM side of a pair summarises its runs: the risk answer is the most frequent one among the runs (yes when $O\le5$; a tie would count as uncertain, and none occurred), and O, S and D are each the lower median over the runs. The summary triple can therefore differ from every individual run. A pair is \emph{definite} when both the deterministic answer and the LLM answer are yes or no, so neither is uncertain. The two answers need not agree for a pair to be definite, and the definite-agreement rate is the share of definite pairs on which they do agree.

\textbf{Repeatability} asks whether repeated runs of the \emph{same evaluator} give the same answer. It involves no deterministic answer, needs at least two valid runs by that evaluator on the pair, and is counted in pairs. An evaluator can be repeatable and still disagree with the deterministic layer, or agree with it on a pair while answering differently from run to run. Neither property shows that the deterministic layer or the LLM is right, because these judgments have no ground truth. Table~\ref{tab:l2denom} lists every count with its unit.

Against the original deterministic baseline of 3 October the severity error is 2.14, and the status and definite counts do not change. By dimension: Integrity agrees in 1 of 12 pairs (the deterministic layer is uncertain on unpinned or unverified local folders, while the LLM answers risk in 10 of 12), Explainability in 10 of 12, and Safety in none (the LLM says risk on all 12 pairs, while the deterministic risk layer never raised a safety risk). On the tampered-weights packet both flag a risk and disagree on severity (deterministic $S=1$, LLM $S=3$). Provider B answered every packet and its counts match the all-runs counts closely (11/36 status, 11/25 definite), so the all-runs figures mostly reflect provider B. The first 22 answers alone (18 pairs) gave status agreement 5/18 and the 38 later answers (24 pairs) 7/24, so completing the experiment did not change the picture.

\textbf{Result, consistency across repeated runs.} Within a single provider, the risk call was identical across runs in 14 of 15 pairs for provider A, 36 of 36 for provider B and 3 of 3 for provider C. The whole triple was identical in 7 of 15 pairs for A, 1 of 36 for B and none of 3 for C. The all-runs column mixes providers (31/36 identical risk calls, 0/36 identical triples) and is shown only as an exploratory measure of consistency, because it mixes between-provider differences with run-to-run noise: provider A rates Integrity less severely than B (mean $O$ 5.33 against 2.59). The provider-specific counts rest on few runs per pair, from uneven allocation, and are descriptive. The comparison therefore shows that a provider can be consistent in its risk call while the triple still varies, and that the providers also differ systematically from each other in how severe they rate the same evidence.

\textbf{What the comparison cannot say.} The three providers differ in model, date and sampling settings, five runs on P01 saw a shortened model card, the prompt cannot withhold a decision, a summary hides disagreement among runs, and 12 packets are few. The comparison describes blind interpretation of fixed evidence. It is inconclusive: it does not show that either the deterministic layer or the LLM is correct.

\section{Reading the disagreements}\label{sec:disagree}
The two interpretation experiments do not say who is right. What they offer is a map of where TrustLens's decision layer and an outside reader part company. This section reads that map, keeping the human review (exploratory, seven submissions) apart from the LLM comparison (60 valid runs from three providers).

\textbf{Human reviewers and TrustLens disagree in three patterns.} First, reviewers saw a risk where TrustLens saw none (P04, P06, P07, P10). In each case the measurement or the text was real, and the rule either had no threshold to apply (P04, P06, P07) or classed the problem as a disclosure gap and not a risk (P10). P07 is the clearest: a model with a severe miss rate of 0.30, better than the reference model's, was judged risky by six of seven reviewers, since a model that lets three in ten severe comments pass is not reassuring even if the baseline is worse. TrustLens had no absolute level to apply. Second, TrustLens withheld a decision where reviewers found the evidence sufficient (P02, P08, P09). The gates behind these cases (interval width, an unverified file listing) encode caution that reviewers did not share, in a study in which no one saw the rule. P02 repeats the point of Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty}: six of seven reviewers read the same interval as evidence of risk. Third, where both saw a risk, they named the same dimension every time, and differed on severity. TrustLens was harsher on a byte mismatch (S=1 against a human median of 3) and milder on fairness and card problems. The reviewers' own rationales explain the first difference: a mismatch shows that bytes differ and not why, and several hedged about a stale manifest.

\textbf{What the pattern implies.} The reviewers' agreement is highest where the question is factual (is the evidence sufficient, which dimension is it) and lowest where it is a judgment of significance (is it a risk, how severe). TrustLens's rules resolve exactly those judgments by thresholds, so it is on those questions that it should be expected to differ from any individual reviewer, and that is where a different threshold would change the outcome. The study cannot say which threshold is better. It does show that the choice is not obvious to a reader who sees only the evidence.

\textbf{The LLM comparison is complete but not decisive.} All 60 planned calls returned valid responses (Section~\ref{sec:l2}), so the missing-data problem of the first sessions is gone. What remains is structural. The LLM prompt offers no way to withhold, so the LLM answered ``risk'' for Safety on every packet while the deterministic layer never raises a safety risk, and the deterministic layer abstains on unverified local folders where the LLM always answers. Exact status agreement (11 of 36 pairs) therefore partly measures different question formats. The 60 runs come from three providers that differ in model, date and sampling settings, and provider B supplied two thirds of them, so the all-runs figures lean on one provider. Consistency across repeated runs is a separate question from agreement with the deterministic layer: within a provider the risk call was nearly always the same, while the whole triple repeated in 7 of provider A's 15 pairs, 1 of provider B's 36 and none of provider C's 3. A provider can therefore be stable in its risk call and still disagree with the deterministic layer, while its exact ratings vary from run to run. The consequence is the design already chosen: the deterministic engine is the default, and every LLM rating carries its source.

\section{External Hugging Face model audit}\label{sec:hub}
\textbf{Tested:} seven Hub models, each pinned to an exact revision and run once on the 3\,000-row evaluation set (282 severe positives). All five probes ran on every model and the evaluated revision equalled the pinned one. \textbf{These are descriptive profiles, not a ranking}, and seven deliberately varied models say nothing about the Hub as a whole. Table~\ref{tab:hubev} answers three questions for each model: what was measured, which findings were raised, and where a rating was withheld.

\textbf{Findings.} A fairness-gap risk was raised on \code{unbiased-toxic-roberta} (TPR gap 0.093, CI lower bound 0.020, only just above $\varepsilon$; the model is described as trained with identity-bias mitigation) and on the dynabench hate-speech model, which was also the only one to trigger the robustness risk (drop 0.072). All seven carry disclosure gaps for safety and card completeness, and two lack a licence in their card metadata. Measured behaviour spans clean accuracy 0.664--0.927, accuracy drop 0.010--0.072, severe FNR 0.089--0.837 and benign false-positive rate 0.018--0.367. The highest miss rate belongs to a hate-speech model whose label scope differs from the toxicity labels, so it measures that mismatch as much as the model.
