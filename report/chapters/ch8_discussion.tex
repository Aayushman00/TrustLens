\chapter{Discussion, Limitations and Ethics}\label{ch:discussion}

\section{What the study shows}
Within its scope, TrustLens does what it set out to do. Its measurements are reproducible to the last digit, they rise with injected defect size in every family swept, and the file check caught a one-byte change that no metadata would show. Separating missing documentation from observed problems cleared the false flags on the clean controls, and the true-positive-gap rule flagged each injected disparity once 40\% of labels were flipped, on data that was not used to build it. Seven real Hub models produced profiles that expose what a download count hides: missing licences, absent safety disclosures, and two models with measurable group gaps. The layers behind the evidence are weaker, and that is the main lesson of the study.

The lesson is that measured evidence, risk decisions and aggregate scores are different things, and improvement in one layer does not guarantee improvement in the others (answers to the research questions are in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}). Evidence is the strongest layer: reproducible, tracking severity in every family swept, and catching what metadata cannot, such as a single flipped byte. The risk layer is conservative by construction, raising a risk only on positive evidence above a stated tolerance. It reacted to fairness defects only from a label-flip rate of 0.4, never graded Robustness or Safety severity in the main sweeps, and raised Integrity and Explainability risks only for a byte mismatch or a contradiction. It also produced the largest systematic difference from human reviewers (P04, P06, P07, P10). Since no acceptable miss rate is defined, when a measurement becomes a risk is a policy choice. The step from evidence to rating is where choices are least constrained by data: each of our fixes there (the range-scaled mapping and the later evidence guards of Section~\ref{sec:osd}) was defensible and could have gone another way. Keeping the layers separate lets a reader see which numbers are observations and which are proposals.

\textbf{The aggregate score is secondary.} It combines proposed ratings by a geometric mean, moved 1.34 points between two identical pilot runs because of the LLM, hides withheld ratings, has a scale relative to our largest injected defect, and has no ground truth to validate against. The decisions made after seeing data are listed in Table~\ref{tab:status}. We did not benchmark against another tool.

\section{Limitations}\label{ch:limits}
The results above hold within these limits.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.1em]
\item \textbf{Task scope and proxies.} Only English-language toxicity classification on Civil Comments was exercised. Each probe measures a proxy (random character swaps, card text, an operational severe subset). Defects are injected, three of nine suite models carry documentation-only defects, and sweeps have four or five levels, one model family and one training seed per condition, so no significance tests are possible.
\item \textbf{Scoring method.} The O/S/D mapping is a proposal fitted to the tested defect range, not calibrated to harm or validated on fresh data. Each dimension is one statistic ($O=S$, $D$ constant). The evidence guards are unvalidated; a hash match shows agreement with a manifest, not absence of a backdoor.
\item \textbf{Fairness groups and rule.} Groups are defined by the annotator-reported identity-attack flag, not identity mentions or protected groups, and this report contains no mention-based rerun. The decision rule looks only at the gap in true-positive rates. A gap in false-positive rates is recorded as evidence but does not reach a decision.
\item \textbf{File coverage in the Hub audit.} Each Hub model's manifest held the one weight file the worker loaded. Other weight files in the repository were not hashed.
\item \textbf{LLM comparison.} All 60 planned calls returned valid responses, but only 22 did so in the first sessions. Of the other 38, 34 were obtained on 28 October, nearly four weeks later, and four (provider C) carry 3 October timestamps. The 60 runs come from three providers with different models and sampling settings (temperature 0.2 recorded for providers B and C, not recorded for A) and very uneven coverage. Provider B supplied 41 runs, so the pooled figures largely reflect B; provider A supplied 15. Provider C supplied 4 and answered only 2 of the 12 packets. The provider-specific repeatability figures are descriptive and should not be read as if the three evaluators had equally extensive evidence. In addition, five runs (all on packet P01) used a shortened prompt because TrustLens cuts the card text and evidence at fixed lengths. Those responses are valid but less comparable with the 55 made on the intended evidence, and P01 enters several packet--dimension pairs. This does not show that the reported agreement rates are wrong. It means that the P01 pairs may be affected by an amount we did not estimate. The prompt cannot withhold a decision and there are 12 packets, so the comparison is inconclusive.
\item \textbf{Human review and Hub audit.} Seven submissions (six under one shared code) on twelve author-chosen packets, with no formal ethics review, are exploratory. The seven Hub models were chosen to vary, ran once and used different rating sources, so their profiles are descriptive.
\item \textbf{Deployment and privacy.} Authentication was removed. The system is for local, single-user use. The LLM-assisted engine sends card text and evidence to third-party providers (Section~\ref{sec:ethics}).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Construct validity.} Each probe is a proxy, and the risk is that a reader takes it for the property. The measured quantities were chosen because they can be computed and reproduced, not because they are the best indicators, and the report names each proxy where its result appears. The Fairness proxy is the weakest. The flag marks comments that annotators judged to contain an identity attack. Such comments are likelier to be toxic, and the label-rate gap of 0.505 shows how closely the two are tied. A finding that recall differs across this flag concerns toxic comments of different kinds and was not tested on comments that mention identity terms.

\textbf{Internal validity.} An experiment can show that a layer responds to a defect only if the defect is the main thing that changed. That held for the nested label flips and for the byte-copy construction of V4b, and it did not hold in the first suite's fairness comparison, where the control was a different model. The fairness rule was therefore developed on data that had failed once. The confirmation on fresh data, with hypotheses H1--H3 and a descriptive H4 written beforehand, limits that concern for the TPR-gap rule but not for the evidence guards, which were set after the external audit. A further concern is that the Fairness defect and the Fairness metric use the same flag, which makes the defect easy to detect and says little about a defect that works through another pathway.

\textbf{External validity.} The behavioural results describe English toxicity classification on one dataset, with one base architecture for the controlled models and label corruptions as the defects. We cannot say whether the layers would respond the same way for sentiment, topic or multilingual classification, where groups, severe subsets and typos may differ. The seven Hub models were chosen to vary and run once, and at least one (\code{unbiased-toxic-roberta}) was trained on data derived from Civil Comments. We did not check for overlap with the evaluation rows.

\textbf{Statistical conclusion validity.} The design supports descriptive statements. A standard deviation of zero over five runs means the same code on the same data and seed gave the same output, which is evidence of determinism, not of low variance across data samples or training seeds. The intervals capture resampling of rows and not retraining. With four or five severity levels a rank correlation of $\pm1$ is a statement about order, not about the shape of a response, and twelve packets with seven reviewers give wide intervals.

\section{Ethical considerations}\label{sec:ethics}
\textbf{Data.} The experiments use the public Civil Comments dataset \cite{borkan2019}, which contains offensive and discriminatory text written by members of the public. Comments were processed by local models. They were not put in a review packet, and the evidence sent to LLM providers consists of card text and aggregate metrics for three dimensions, not dataset rows. The group flag is a coarse annotator-averaged signal. No inference about individuals is made.

\textbf{Volunteer review.} Reviewers were volunteers who filled in an anonymous online form. They saw card text and numbers, not comments, entered only an assigned reviewer code, and gave no name, email address or demographic information. The raw export is kept unmodified. The study is small and informal, we did not seek formal ethics approval and none is documented, and the report does not claim it.

\textbf{Disclosure to LLM providers.} The LLM-assisted engine sends the model-card text and probe evidence to third-party providers. For public Hub models this discloses nothing that is not already public. For a private or pre-release model it would disclose the card and measured behaviour to a hosted service, whose retention we did not examine. The default engine sends nothing.

\textbf{Misreading the dashboard.} A single score is easy to read as a certificate. The scores in this report separate synthetic defect levels and are not calibrated to harm, and a withheld rating is not a pass. The dashboard therefore leads with the five-dimension profile and shows the aggregate last, labels every proposed rating ``PROPOSED / REQUIRES VALIDATION'', makes withheld ratings visible and states whether a human reviewed the numbers. If the thresholds were used as a gate, a model author could tune to clear them without becoming more trustworthy. TrustLens should not be used to approve, certify or rank models (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).
