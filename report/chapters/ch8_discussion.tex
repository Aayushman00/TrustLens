Three things stand out. First, the evidence layer is the strongest part of the system: it is reproducible, it tracks severity, and it catches things that metadata cannot, such as a byte flip. Second, the risk layer is conservative by construction, since a risk is only raised on positive evidence of a problem and above a stated tolerance. That conservatism produced the largest systematic difference from human reviewers (Section~\ref{sec:disc-human}). Third, the score layer is where the arbitrariness lives, since this is where a proposal turns evidence into a rating, and every one of our fixes there (v4, v9) made a defensible choice that could have gone another way. We think anyone who builds a FRIES tool will meet this same step and should expect it to be the hardest one to defend.

\section{Why fairness was hard, and what the final rule is and is not}
Fairness took four attempts. The first binary path could not raise a risk. The second (excess DPD) failed its own inspection because it depended on base rates. The TPR-gap rule that replaced it passed the confirmatory test on defect detection (H2, H3) but failed on controls (H1), and its margin between an injected defect (EOD 0.167 at flip 0.4) and a real model's own disparity (0.145) is small. We take three things from this.

(i) A fairness rule that reads a disparity cannot tell whether the disparity was injected, inherited from the data or caused by the model, and the Civil Comments identity groups have base rates of 0.11 against 0.64, which makes the question unanswerable from predictions alone. The rule's status text states it as ``evidence for review''. We think that is the right claim.
(ii) The width gate is a significant degree of freedom. Five of the six L5 CI widths lie between 0.145 and 0.156, so a threshold at 0.15 decides outcomes. We did not retune it, which is consistent with our rules but means the gate's value, which was inherited, is arbitrary and the clean control's non-flag is an artefact of a 0.003 margin.
(iii) The metric was chosen after we saw the exploratory data. L5 is a legitimate confirmation of defect detection precisely because it used fresh data and a new seed, but it is a single data seed and a single training seed, and training-seed variance was not measured.

\section{Human reviewers and TrustLens disagree in a consistent direction}\label{sec:disc-human}
The disagreement table (Table~\ref{tab:l10tl}) is not noise. Four of the twelve packets (P04, P06, P07, P10) are cases where the evidence layer measured something and the risk layer said no risk, while the human plurality said risk. In those packets the underlying reason is similar: TrustLens has no behavioural safety risk rule at all, and its robustness tolerance (an accuracy drop of 0.05) is not reached by the 0.030 drop in P04, so it raises no risk. Reviewers, shown ``severe-harm FNR 0.646 against a reference at 0.40'', judged it a risk. The Claude supplement shows the same pattern (risk on 12 of 12 safety packets against none from TrustLens), and so does the deterministic-versus-LLM comparison (Safety 0 of 6 agreement). We do not conclude that TrustLens is wrong; the packets do not say what an acceptable miss rate is, and the UNCERTAIN that Claude gave on the safety control made exactly that point. We conclude that a decision about when behaviour becomes a risk is a policy choice that the framework has so far left implicit, and that reports should show the measured number next to a reference rather than imply that ``no risk raised'' means ``fine''.

In the other direction, TrustLens abstained on two packets (P02, P09) where reviewers found the evidence sufficient, via the CI-width and listing-unverified gates. The gates are conservative, and one is a threshold we did not tune. That is acceptable for an audit tool, but it means abstention rates are a function of gate values and should be reported with them.

On severity, humans and TrustLens differ by two or more points on three packets, but humans agree with each other only moderately (Krippendorff's $\alpha=0.60$, 35\% exact). A framework whose severity numbers differ from reviewers' by less than the reviewers differ from each other would be doing about as well as can be measured; here we cannot say that, and we do not claim it.

\section{Deterministic versus LLM interpretation}
We kept the deterministic engine as the default for a reason that the L2 data underline. Where the LLM answered, the exact $O/S/D$ triple never repeated across runs (0 of 12 pairs), and the answers depended on which provider happened to be reachable (Integrity $O$ 5.67 from one provider, 2.85 from the other). For a tool whose output is meant to be audited, an interpretation step that changes with the provider, quota or time of day is a weak foundation. The LLM does see information the deterministic path does not: it reads a card's prose, and it noticed content problems that a keyword section detector cannot. On the other hand, the deterministic engine is blind to exactly those problems, and reviewers' rationales show it matters (a card with the right headings and no content). We therefore treat \code{llm\_v1} as an experimental comparison engine, report which source rated each aspect, and do not claim either engine is better. Operationally, the dependence on three free-tier providers is a risk of its own: in the L2 study 38 of 60 runs, and in the first real-model run 14 of 15, were never answered by a model.

\section{Why the aggregate score is secondary}
The aggregate FRIES number is a function of ratings that are proposals, combined by a geometric mean with equal weights. Our data give four direct reasons not to read it as more than a summary.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{It moves with the LLM.} In the pilot the original reference's overall score fell 1.34 points between two runs with identical inputs.
\item \textbf{It hides abstention.} A model with a withheld dimension has no score, while a model whose dimension was filled by the fallback has one. Two scores can therefore differ in how much of the evidence stands behind them (Table~\ref{tab:hubprof}).
\item \textbf{Its scale is relative.} Under v4 a three-point accuracy drop is the bottom of the scale; under v3 the same drop (eight swaps) scored 7.96. Neither number says how bad three points is.
\item \textbf{It cannot be validated here.} We have no ground truth for trust. The reviewer study checks evidence reading, not the score.
\end{itemize}
For these reasons the report shows the profile first. The profile can be read dimension by dimension, and the evidence behind each entry can be inspected.
