\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

\section{Background and problem statement}
Most people who need a toxicity classifier or a sentiment model today do not train one. They search the Hugging Face Hub, skim the first lines of a model card, glance at the download count and call \code{from\_pretrained}. That workflow is quick, and it is also the point where trust gets decided almost by accident. The Hub holds a very large number of checkpoints uploaded by anyone with an account, and an empirical study of the registry found that metadata and documentation are often incomplete or inconsistent \cite{jiang2023reuse}. A model card can be an untouched template. A licence field can be empty, or can contradict the card text. A card can call a model ``production ready, no known biases'' while the model's behaviour on identity-related text says something different.

The question this project works on is therefore a narrow one: \emph{given a model reference and a small labelled dataset, can a tool collect evidence about the model in a reproducible, inspectable way, and show a reviewer where the risks are and where the evidence is too thin to say?} We are not asking whether a model is ``trustworthy'' in an absolute sense. Probes built in one academic year cannot answer that; this report is about what they can and cannot see.

\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.92\textwidth}{\small\textbf{Central framing.} TrustLens is an evidence-driven, FRIES-aligned model auditing framework. The five-dimensional audit profile is the primary output; the aggregate FRIES score is only a secondary heuristic summary and must not be presented as a universally validated measure of trustworthiness.}}
\end{center}



\section{Motivation}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Trust is multi-dimensional.} Accuracy says nothing about behaviour across groups, stability under noisy input, whether the weights are the published ones, or whether the documentation is honest \cite{kemmerzell2025,mattioli2024}.
\item \textbf{Existing scoring ideas leave the hard step open.} The FRIES score \cite{rutinowski2024} returns one 0--10 number from an FMEA-style analysis, but leaves the step from measured evidence to Occurrence, Severity and Detection ratings to experts. A tool has to define that step and say how weak the definition is.
\item \textbf{A single number invites over-reading.} We wanted every entry in a report to point at stored evidence and to say what it does not mean.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Objectives}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Build a working audit pipeline for Hugging Face text-classification models: import, local inference, five probes, O/S/D assessment, scoring, review and report.
\item Keep the pipeline honest: insufficient evidence is recorded as such rather than turned into a number, documentation gaps are not reported as defects, and every evaluation carries its methodology version.
\item Test the tool on models with controlled defects, independent reviewers and real pinned Hub models (the research questions below), and report negative findings next to the successes.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research questions}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2em,style=nextline]
\item[RQ1] Does the implemented pipeline collect the evidence it claims to collect, reproducibly?
\item[RQ2] When a defect of known severity is injected into a model, do TrustLens's measured evidence, its risk decision and its score each respond, and do they respond in proportion to severity?
\item[RQ3] Where the decision layers fail to respond, can the failure be fixed without tuning against the data that exposed it?
\item[RQ4] How does deterministic interpretation of evidence compare with LLM-based interpretation of the same evidence?
\item[RQ5] Do independent evidence reviewers agree with each other, and with TrustLens?
\item[RQ6] What does an audit profile look like for real Hub models, and what happens when the evidence is insufficient?
\end{description}
None asks whether TrustLens can tell a trustworthy model from an untrustworthy one in general; our data could not answer that.

\section{Contributions}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2em,style=nextline]
\item[Engineering.] A FastAPI, Celery and React system with local inference, a content-addressed evidence store, versioned reports, and a backend test suite of 913 collected tests (Chapter~\ref{ch:testing}).
\item[Methodological.] Separate evidence, risk and score layers; explicit abstention; risks split from \emph{disclosure gaps}; an equal-opportunity fairness rule with a bootstrap gate; artifact-set integrity; a range-normalised O/S/D mapping; blinded evidence packets.
\item[Empirical.] Controlled flawed-model suites with severity sweeps, a pre-registered confirmatory fairness run, a deterministic-versus-LLM comparison, a human evidence-review study and an audit of seven real Hub models.
\end{description}

\section{Claims we do not make}
TrustLens does not certify, approve or rank any model; the seven Hub models have descriptive profiles only. The FRIES score is not validated against human trust judgments, and the O/S/D mapping is a proposal. The reviewer study is not a validation (reviewers are evidence readers; the sample is small). Results cover one task (English toxicity), one dataset family (Civil Comments) and mostly BERT-family models.
