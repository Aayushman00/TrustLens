\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

\section{Background and problem statement}
Most people who need a toxicity classifier or a sentiment model today do not train one. They search the Hugging Face Hub, skim the first lines of a model card, glance at the download count and call \code{from\_pretrained}. That workflow is quick, and it is also the point where trust gets decided almost by accident. The Hub holds a very large number of checkpoints uploaded by anyone with an account, and an empirical study of the registry found that metadata and documentation are often incomplete or inconsistent \cite{jiang2023reuse}. A model card can be an untouched template. A licence field can be empty, or can contradict the card text. A card can call a model ``production ready, no known biases'' while the model's behaviour on identity-related text says something different.

The question this project works on is therefore a narrow one: \emph{given a model reference and a small labelled dataset, can a tool collect evidence about the model in a reproducible, inspectable way, and show a reviewer where the risks are and where the evidence is too thin to say?} We are not asking whether a model is ``trustworthy'' in an absolute sense. A set of probes we could build in one academic year cannot answer that, and most of this report is about being honest regarding what the probes can and cannot see.

\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.92\textwidth}{\small\textbf{Central framing.} TrustLens is an evidence-driven, FRIES-aligned model auditing framework. The five-dimensional audit profile is the primary output; the aggregate FRIES score is only a secondary heuristic summary and must not be presented as a universally validated measure of trustworthiness.}}
\end{center}

We keep returning to this statement. It decides how results are reported (per dimension first, aggregate last), how abstention is treated (an abstention is a result, not a failure to produce a number) and which claims we refuse to make.

\section{Motivation}
Three observations pushed the project in this direction.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Trust is multi-dimensional.} Accuracy says nothing about behaviour across groups, stability under noisy input, whether the weights are the ones that were published, whether the documentation is honest, or what happens when the model misses severe content. The trustworthy-AI literature groups these concerns in several overlapping ways \cite{kemmerzell2025,mattioli2024}.
\item \textbf{Existing scoring ideas leave the hard step open.} The FRIES score of Rutinowski et~al.\ \cite{rutinowski2024} returns one 0--10 number from an FMEA-style analysis, but the step from measured evidence to the Occurrence, Severity and Detection ratings is left to experts. A tool that applies the formula has to define that step, and has to say how weak the definition is.
\item \textbf{A single number invites over-reading.} A figure that looks authoritative is dangerous when it cannot be traced. We wanted every entry in a TrustLens report to point at stored evidence, and wanted the report to say what the entry does not mean.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Objectives}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Build a working audit pipeline for Hugging Face text-classification models: import, local inference, five probes, O/S/D assessment, scoring, review and report.
\item Keep the pipeline honest. Missing or insufficient evidence is recorded as such rather than turned into a number, documentation gaps are not reported as defects, and every evaluation carries the version of the methodology that produced it.
\item Test the tool against models whose defects we control, and measure separately whether the \emph{evidence}, the \emph{risk decision} and the \emph{score} react.
\item Ask whether independent readers of the same evidence reach the same conclusions as TrustLens, and where they do not.
\item Apply the tool to real, pinned Hub models and report the resulting profiles without ranking them.
\item Report negative findings, provider failures and abandoned designs next to the successes.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research questions}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2em,style=nextline]
\item[RQ1] Does the implemented pipeline collect the evidence it claims to collect, reproducibly?
\item[RQ2] When a defect of known severity is injected into a model, do TrustLens's measured evidence, its risk decision and its score each respond, and do they respond in proportion to severity?
\item[RQ3] Where the decision layers fail to respond, can the failure be fixed without tuning against the data that exposed it?
\item[RQ4] How does deterministic interpretation of evidence compare with LLM-based interpretation of the same evidence?
\item[RQ5] Do independent evidence reviewers agree with each other, and with TrustLens?
\item[RQ6] What does an audit profile look like for real Hub models, and what happens when the evidence is insufficient?
\end{description}
None of these asks whether TrustLens can tell a trustworthy model from an untrustworthy one in general. We do not think our data could answer that question.

\section{Contributions}
We keep three kinds of contribution apart on purpose.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2em,style=nextline]
\item[Engineering.] A FastAPI, Celery and React system with local inference, a content-addressed evidence store, versioned reports, and a backend test suite of 881 collected tests (Chapter~\ref{ch:testing}).
\item[Methodological.] A strict separation of measured evidence (Layer~A), risk assessment (Layer~B) and score (Layer~C); explicit evidence statuses and abstention; a split between \emph{risks} and \emph{disclosure gaps}; an equal-opportunity fairness rule with a bootstrap gate; artifact-set integrity; a range-normalised O/S/D mapping; and a protocol for blinded evidence packets.
\item[Empirical.] Controlled flawed-model suites with severity sweeps, a pre-registered confirmatory fairness run, a deterministic-versus-LLM comparison, a human evidence-review study and an audit of seven real Hub models, including the cases where TrustLens did not do what we hoped.
\end{description}
We do not claim a new trust metric. The FRIES formula comes from earlier work. Our contribution is the surrounding machinery and the testing of it.

\section{Claims we do not make}
So that nobody has to infer them, here are the claims this report does \emph{not} make.
\begin{itemize}
\item TrustLens does not certify, approve or rank any model. The seven Hub models in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} have descriptive profiles only.
\item The FRIES score is not validated against human judgments of trust, and the O/S/D mapping is a proposal, not an established science.
\item The reviewer study is not a validation of TrustLens. Reviewers are evidence readers, not ground truth, and the sample is small and partly non-independent.
\item Results cover one task (English toxicity classification), one dataset family (Civil Comments) and mostly BERT-family models. Nothing here generalises to images, audio or generative models.
\item A matching artifact hash does not show that a model is safe, only that the bytes are the ones a manifest describes.
\end{itemize}
