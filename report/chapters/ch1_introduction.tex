\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

\section{Problem and gap}
Most people who need a toxicity classifier don't train one. They search the Hugging Face Hub, skim a model card, look at the download count and call \code{from\_pretrained}. That's where trust gets decided, and it's close to an accident. Anyone with an account can upload a checkpoint, so the Hub holds a huge number of them, and one empirical study found their metadata and documentation are often incomplete or inconsistent \cite{jiang2023reuse}. A card might be an untouched template. A licence field might be empty or contradict the card. A card can even say ``no known biases'' while the model's behaviour on identity-related text says the opposite.

Auditing a model like this is hard, for three reasons. Trust has several dimensions that accuracy doesn't capture \cite{kemmerzell2025,mattioli2024}. The model card is the only standard disclosure, and it's voluntary and unverified. Existing scoring ideas also skip the difficult step. The FRIES trust score \cite{rutinowski2024} combines Occurrence, Severity and Detection (O/S/D) ratings into one 0--10 number, but it leaves the jump from \emph{measured evidence} to those ratings to experts. A tool has to define that jump, and then show how weak the definition is.

This project asks one narrow question: \emph{given a model reference and a small labelled dataset, can a tool collect evidence about the model reproducibly, and show a reviewer where the risks are and where the evidence is too thin to say anything?} We don't ask whether a model is ``trustworthy'' in an absolute sense. We only describe what our tests can see.

The percentile bootstrap draws the evaluation rows with replacement 1\,000 times, recomputes the metric each time and takes the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. These intervals reflect resampling of rows from one evaluation set. They do not capture variation from retraining a model or from a different dataset.\\

Synthetic defect; clean control & A defect we inject on purpose (for example, relabelling 40\% of some training comments) so that the correct answer is known. A clean control is a comparable model trained without the defect.\\
Evidence packet; mode & An evidence packet is a self-contained bundle of evidence about one model, used to compare how people, an LLM and TrustLens read the same facts. TrustLens runs in one of two modes: automatic (the system finalises ratings without a person) or human-review (it stops and waits for a reviewer to confirm or change them).\\

\section{Research questions}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.6em,itemsep=0.1em]
\item[RQ1] Does the pipeline collect the evidence it claims to collect, reproducibly?
\item[RQ2] When a defect of known severity is injected into a model, do the measured evidence, the risk decision and the score each respond, and in proportion to severity?
\item[RQ3] Where the decision layers fail to respond, can the failure be fixed without tuning against the data that exposed it?
\item[RQ4] How does deterministic interpretation of evidence compare with LLM-based interpretation of the same evidence?
\item[RQ5] Do independent evidence reviewers agree with each other, and with TrustLens?
\item[RQ6] What does an audit profile look like for real Hub models, and what happens when the evidence is insufficient?
\end{description}

\section{Contributions}
TrustLens puts known techniques together under the FRIES formula: group-fairness metrics, a perturbation test, a miss-rate test, model-card checks and file hashing. We don't claim any new algorithms. What we contribute is an applied framework, plus an empirical look at how its evidence, decision and scoring layers behave:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{An integrated audit workflow} for Hugging Face text-classification models, organised around Fairness, Robustness, Integrity, Explainability and Safety, from model import to a versioned report.
\item \textbf{Separation of measured evidence, risk decisions and ratings}, with explicit withholding of a rating when evidence is insufficient.
\item \textbf{Missing documentation treated separately} from evidence of an observed problem (``disclosure gaps'': information a model card should give but does not).
\item \textbf{Controlled experiments} on repeatability, defect detection, score sensitivity and decision behaviour, including negative results.
\item \textbf{Hash-based integrity checking and evidence provenance}: the files listed in a model's reference manifest are compared with their reference hashes (in the external audit the manifest held the one weight file the worker loaded, so other files in each repository were not checked), every probe output is stored under its SHA-256 hash, and every evaluation records the exact model version, dataset, label mapping, settings and version of the scoring method it used.
\item \textbf{An evaluation} with controlled flawed models, blinded evidence reviewers and seven real Hub models.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and intended use}\label{sec:scope}
TrustLens is a research prototype for local, single-user use. We ran the experiments on the experimental version of the prototype between 2 and 28 October 2026, and the repository has changed since then. Appendix~\ref{app:trace} says which stored results, scripts and versions belong to which experiment. The behavioural experiments cover one task only, English-language toxicity classification on Civil Comments. The five-dimensional audit profile is the main output, and the aggregate FRIES score is a secondary summary. The typical user is a team choosing a toxicity classifier from the Hub. They have a download count and a model card, and they want evidence beyond both. TrustLens helps a human reviewer see where the evidence is strong and where it is thin. Section~\ref{ch:limits} lists everything it doesn't do. It isn't a certification, ranking or deployment-approval tool, and a measured property is evidence about trust and never trust itself.

Chapter~\ref{ch:lit} reviews related work. Chapters~\ref{ch:arch} and~\ref{ch:fries} describe the system and the method, and Chapter~\ref{ch:method} defines the experiments. Chapter~\ref{ch:example} follows one stored audit from measurement to report. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} gives the results, and Chapter~\ref{ch:cases} looks closely at three of the audited models. Chapters~\ref{ch:discussion} to~\ref{ch:conclusion} cover interpretation, threats to validity, ethics, reproducibility and conclusions. The appendices hold detailed results, the LLM prompts and reviewer protocol, a reproducibility summary and extra figures.
